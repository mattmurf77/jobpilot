# What Makes Prose Read as Human, and How to De-AI a Draft (with a Cover-Letter Checklist)

*Compiled 2026-08-04 by research agent, web research (2024-2026 sources preferred; classic craft sources included as foundational).*

## 1. Craft fundamentals (the enduring rules AI drafts violate)

- **Sentence rhythm, varied first/last.** Verlyn Klinkenborg, *Several Short Sentences About Writing* (Sivers summary, accessed 2026): "Pay attention to rhythm, first and last." His diagnostic is auditory — "The ear is smarter than the eye... Read until your ear detects a problem, a subtle disturbance" (sive.rs/book/ShortSentences). AI drafts fail this because sentence length clusters near a mean; human prose swings between short and long.
- **Concrete over abstract.** Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (cited via lifeclub.org summary, 2025): insists on "fresh wording and concrete imagery over familiar verbiage and abstract summary," and traces this to George Orwell's *Politics and the English Language* critique of "pompous abstraction that obscures human realities."
- **Active voice, used deliberately not dogmatically.** Pinker's nuance (same source): passive constructions "have indispensable functions" for directing reader attention — the rule is "prefer active," not "ban passive."
- **The read-aloud test** recurs across both classic and 2025 sources: Klinkenborg above, and the 2025 practitioner guide from WriteHybrid ("How to Humanize AI Text," writehybrid.com, 2026) — "Read the whole passage out loud once without editing, and mark every place you stumble... Those marks are your edit list."
- **Ann Handley, *Everybody Writes*** (summarized via Shortform, 2025 edition notes): the "Go, Push, Shine" process — write an "ugly first draft," then explicitly "add your voice," then "do a robot edit, do a human edit, read it out loud." Handley's rule: "writing must feel like it's written by a human who relates to and understands the audience," achieved through specificity over vague terms.

## 2. Human voice markers (what a detector — human or algorithmic — actually keys on)

- **Stylometric research, Nature Humanities & Social Sciences Communications** (2025, "Stylometric comparisons of human versus AI-generated creative writing"): AI models produce "compact, predictable" styles; human writing shows "far greater stylistic range, shaped by personal voice, creative intent, and individual experience" — i.e., genuine unevenness is a *feature*, not sloppiness.
- **Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism** (2025, "How AI-generated prose diverges from human writing and why it matters"): documents specific AI tells — overuse of "delve," "resonate," "navigate," "commendable," "meticulous"; systematic avoidance of contractions and slang ("gonna," "wanna"); and "formulaic emotional statements" like "X isn't just a tool, it's a revolution." Journalist Alex Mahadevan's summary line: AI prose is "a lot of words that don't say anything... we always say, show, don't tell."
- **First-person plainness and idiosyncrasy**: the same Nature piece and UCC/techxplore coverage of it (Dec 2025) converge on "even when ChatGPT tries to sound human, its writing still carries a detectable fingerprint" — the antidote is not more polish but *more specific, only-you-could-know detail* (a real project name, a real number, a real awkward moment), because AI cannot originate lived-experience specifics.

## 3. De-AI-ification techniques from editors (2025-2026, substantive sources)

The most concrete, non-SEO-spam source found: **"How I edit AI-generated copy and why it takes so long"** (wonderingaboutai.substack.com, 2025) gives a repeatable **SHARP framework** plus real before/afters:

1. **Spot hallucinations** — verify every stat, quote, citation before touching style.
2. **Hone your voice** — strip clichés and three specific AI-formula patterns:
   - *Em-dash stacking*: Before: "Automation helps teams scale — saving time and resources — while maintaining personalization — all without adding headcount." After: "Automation helps teams scale and save resources while maintaining personalization. No additional headcount required."
   - *Set-up/punchline*: Before: "The problem? Integration complexity." After: "Integration complexity blocks progress."
   - *False contrast ("it's not X, it's Y")*: Before: "It's not about having more data, it's about having the right data." After: "The right data matters more than simply having a lot of it."
   - *Explainer verbs* ("dive deep," "unpack," "delve"): Before: "Let's dive deep into why retention metrics matter." After: "Retention metrics matter because they predict revenue. Here are the three that actually move the needle."
3. **Add unique insight** — inject a perspective/experience an AI cannot replicate.
4. **Read it aloud** — test pacing.
5. **Rest, then do a final review** with fresh eyes.

A companion post, **"Stop Sounding Like ChatGPT: An Editor's Cleanup System"** (louisbouchard.substack.com, 2025), independently names the same failure cluster — "over-neat structure," "awkward transitions," "polished-but-vague phrasing," excess em-dashes — confirming these are the first things a professional editor targets, before line-level wordsmithing.

## 4. Cover-letter-specific voice guidance

- **Ask a Manager (Alison Green)** publishes real successful letters with editorial commentary; her recurring standard, e.g. in the Jan 2025 "career changer" letter and the 2023 before/after post, is that the letter must be "the writer's voice... not yours" and that copying structure defeats the point because letters work *because* they're personal and non-transferable.
- Career-advice sources converge on concrete substitutions: replace "I would like to express my sincere interest" with something closer to plain speech ("This job looks interesting" / "I think I'd be a good fit") — flagged across multiple 2025-2026 practitioner guides (coverlettercopilot.ai; remotestack.in, 2026) as the single most common tell, alongside generic phrases like "results-oriented professional" and "proven track record" replacing them with one specific company detail plus one measurable, named achievement. (Note: a widely repeated "89% of AI cover letters" statistic traces to SEO-blog secondary sourcing, not a verifiable HBR study — treat as directional marketing claim, not fact.)

## 5. The 15-point humanize checklist

*(Examples below are invented/neutral, not drawn from any real letter.)*

1. **Read it aloud first.** Mark every stumble. *Before:* "I am writing to express my interest in..." *After:* "I want this job." (Klinkenborg; WriteHybrid 2026)
2. **Cut throat-clearing openers.** *Before:* "I am excited to submit my application for the Marketing Manager role." *After:* "I've spent six years turning small marketing budgets into outsized results — this role is the next one." (Zinsser tradition; Ask a Manager)
3. **Kill em-dash stacking.** *Before:* "This role fits me — combining strategy — and hands-on execution — perfectly." *After:* "This role combines strategy and hands-on execution, which is exactly how I like to work." (SHARP framework, wonderingaboutai 2025)
4. **Remove false-contrast formula.** *Before:* "It's not about having more leads, it's about having the right leads." *After:* "The right leads matter more than lead volume." (SHARP)
5. **Replace explainer verbs** (delve, unpack, dive deep, navigate). *Before:* "I want to delve into how I can help your team navigate growth." *After:* "Here's how I'd help your team grow." (Reuters Institute 2025)
6. **Swap abstraction for one concrete detail only you know.** *Before:* "I have a proven track record of success." *After:* "Last quarter I rebuilt our onboarding flow and cut drop-off from 40% to 22%." (Pinker; Nature 2025)
7. **Vary sentence length on purpose.** *Before:* three medium sentences in a row, same length. *After:* one short sentence punches after two longer ones. (Klinkenborg)
8. **Use "I did X," not "I was responsible for X."** *Before:* "I was responsible for leading the redesign." *After:* "I led the redesign." (Handley; active-voice consensus)
9. **Allow one sentence fragment or one "And"/"But" opener.** *Before:* "Additionally, I bring strong collaboration skills." *After:* "And I like the messy parts of collaboration — the disagreements, not just the wins." (practitioner guides, 2025-2026)
10. **Drop hedging on real opinions.** *Before:* "I believe this could potentially be a good fit." *After:* "This is the job I've been angling for." (craft consensus)
11. **Use contractions where a person would.** *Before:* "I do not believe there is a better match." *After:* "I don't think there's a better match." (Reuters Institute — AI systematically avoids contractions)
12. **Replace generic praise of the company with one specific, checkable fact.** *Before:* "I admire your company's innovative culture." *After:* "Your team's shift to a four-day trial run last year is the kind of experiment I want to help run more of." (Ask a Manager pattern)
13. **Let paragraphs be uneven.** *Before:* four paragraphs, each 4 sentences. *After:* one 2-sentence paragraph after a longer one, for emphasis. (Nature 2025 stylometric finding — variability is a marker of human authorship)
14. **Cut the summary-of-resume close.** *Before:* "As you can see from my resume, I have the skills listed above." *After:* "If half of this sounds like your team's actual problems, let's talk." (Ask a Manager, HBR consensus)
15. **Rest and re-read once before sending.** No AI shortcut here — the SHARP framework's final step is simply time + fresh eyes, which catches leftover clichés no rule-based pass will. (wonderingaboutai.substack.com 2025)
